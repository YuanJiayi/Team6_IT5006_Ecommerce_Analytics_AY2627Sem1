IT5006 Team 6 · Phase 2 proposal

Promise faster, warn earlier

We promise customers 22 days for parcels that arrive in about 9. One regression model lets us promise 5 days sooner at almost the same reliability. One classification model then flags orders that will miss that promise, before the customer finds out.

The problem

  • The promise is padded. In the most recent months the median order arrived 13 days before its promised date. Customers see a long wait at checkout.
  • Padding buys nothing. Orders 4 to 7 days early and 15 to 21 days early get the same review score (4.2 to 4.3 stars).
  • Missing the promise is costly. 61% of late orders get a 1 to 2 star review. The review request goes out on the promised date, so late customers review before the parcel arrives.

The two models along an order

At checkout

Delivery-time model Regression

Predicts
Days to delivery, as seller handling time plus carrier time. A safety margin, reset daily from recent deliveries, turns it into the promised date.
Uses
Recent carrier times on the route, current route volume, distance, the seller's usual speed and unshipped backlog.
Who uses it
Automatic. The customer sees the date. Logistics sets the reliability target.
Result
Promises 5 days shorter than today at 95.9% vs 96.5% on time. Beats the best simple rule on both length and reliability (17.1 vs 17.6 days, 95.9% vs 94.8%). Average error 3.9 days vs 4.9 for the rule.
At carrier handover

Late-warning model Classification

Predicts
Will this order miss its promised date? Yes or no, with a probability.
Uses
The same route and seller signals, measured against the days left before the promise.
Who uses it
Customer service, as a daily list: chase the carrier, and message the highest-risk customers before the review request arrives.
Result
A list of 10% of shipments catches 62% of late orders, typically 1 to 3 days before the promised date. A random list catches 10%. The same model, re-run at handover, also updates the customer's arrival estimate (average error 2.6 days).

What we are not claiming

  • The data has no browsing or abandoned-cart records, so it cannot show that shorter promises sell more. We propose an A/B test.
  • On the warning list, the model performs about the same as a simple rule. It earns its place by supporting the decision and setting list size by probability, not by beating the rule.
  • The safety margin learns from orders 45 to 90 days old, so it reacts slowly to sudden slowdowns. In the Feb to Mar 2018 slowdown, on-time fell to 91.7%. Deployment needs a monitor and a peak-season buffer.
  • Results cover delivered orders only, from one 3-month test period. Models are retrained monthly.

Why this version, not Pratik's

Pratik's models estimate how long delivery will take, at checkout and at handover. That is useful, but it changes no decision and is not compared with today's promise. Our model sets the promise itself and beats today's on promise length. At handover, on the same 19,230 orders, our model's error is 2.6 days against 3.9 for Pratik's, with less bias (+0.9 vs +2.8 days). On earlier validation months the gap was much smaller (0.2 days), so we claim at least as good, and better on test.
