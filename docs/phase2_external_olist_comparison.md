# What stronger Olist delivery-risk results actually measure

This note checks the authors' published code and result artifacts, not just headline scores. Our reference task is **lateness among eventually delivered orders**, using the actual and promised **calendar dates**, with a prediction immediately after checkout/payment submission and a **later-purchase** test period. Our test has 3.48% late orders, ROC-AUC 0.668 and average precision (AP, reported as PR-AUC) 0.065. These public projects use the Kaggle copy of Olist; our input CSVs came from Canvas. The methods matter more than a direct score ranking.

## What this suggests for our work

1. **Test boosting on our own five chronological validation windows**, with our unchanged calendar-date label, checkout feature contract and fold-specific preprocessing. Compare AP with logistic regression per window; keep the later-period test out of model selection. Public results make boosting plausible, not guaranteed: Olist-atraso's tuning did not transfer, and DelaySentinel's histogram booster lost to logistic regression.
2. **Do not import post-checkout features** such as current-order approval delay or carrier handover time. They can produce attractive results while answering a different business question. A seller's *past* handover speed or past route history is a legitimate candidate only when built from events already known at each purchase.
3. **Keep the natural later-period late rate in evaluation.** Random stratification, full-timestamp labels and different cutoff dates can all raise the reported test late rate; this audit cannot assign a share of the difference to each cause. Several public test sets have 5–8% late orders rather than our 3.48%. Their raw AP values cannot be compared directly to ours; report each score beside its own prevalence, label rule and split.
4. **Separate ranking from alert policy.** Even a 0.81 ROC-AUC project chose its family and threshold with test labels; a 0.768 project caught only one late order at its displayed threshold. Our report should show how many orders staff must review to catch a given share of late deliveries, and make clear that any newly chosen rule needs fresh, untouched data for a final claim.

These checks explain why the most impressive public numbers do **not** establish a reachable score for our exact task. They also leave room for a real improvement from boosting or better point-in-time history; only our own chronological validation can show whether those changes help.
